Once Upon an Entrepreneur | Zach Wahls

Zach Wahls | Founder, Scouts for Equality 

Zach Wahls is a founder of Scouts for Equality as well as an author, speaker and advocate. He also co-created, "The Woman Cards," with his sister and funded their Kickstarter within 3.5 hours.

Occupation: Graduate Student

Tell us about your background. Where were you born/raised and what has influenced you towards the path you’re on?

I was born in Wisconsin and raised in Iowa. I'd say the biggest influences in my life have been my parents, who taught me early on that if I wanted spending money, I'd have to work for it. As a result, I was always on the lookout for ways to create value for other people in exchange for the money I wanted to spend on Pokemon cards or laser tag or whatever.

My mother Terry often told me a story about her father John, who started a small trucking business after returning home from his time in the US Army. In the beginning, he was picky about the jobs he took and would rarely take long or inconvenient jobs. Before too long, his business was on the verge of going under. He started taking the tough jobs and threw himself into the work. Not only did his business rebound, it wound up growing dramatically to more trucks and several employees. Soon, Grandpa John was able to sell the business and purchase two-hundred acres of his own in northeast Iowa to start the family farm on which he raised my mother and her brothers. It's a simple story, but it has stuck with me.

When did the moment spark, if at all, where you decided to pursue your path?

In 2011, I was invited to deliver a short speech before the Iowa House Judiciary Committee about growing up with gay parents during a hearing on same-sex marriage in our state. My speech was recorded and uploaded to YouTube, where it went on to become YouTube's most-watched political video of the year.

It wasn't so much that I caught lightning in a bottle so much as lightning landed in a bottle and then rolled into my lap. To be clear, there was certainly some initiative involved on my part, but it is not lost on me that luck played an enormous factor in everything that happened. I understood that I had been given fifteen minutes and that I had to decide if I would let my time expire or if I'd use those fifteen minutes to extend the clock. I knew that doing so would open me and my family up to more exposure, but that our family story could win a lot of hearts and minds. After long conversations with my family and some close mentors, I decided to extend the clock.

This turned my life upside down. I dropped out of my civil/environmental engineering program at the University of Iowa to travel, speak, and write about LGBTQ rights as a full time advocate. I signed a book deal, joined a speakers bureau, and spoke all over the country in support of different groups to help raise money. The next year, I co-founded and became the inaugural executive director of Scouts for Equality, a small group of former Boy Scouts that led the fight against the Boy Scouts of America's ban on gay youth and adults. We won that campaign in about three years. (I managed to finish my bachelor's at Iowa along the way.) The path I've been on since I delivered that speech has been long and tough and often inconvenient, but it's opened truly incredibly opportunities.

When did the moment spark, if at all, where you knew you had chosen the right path?

Being a full time advocate at the time most of my friends were partying three to four days a week wasn't easy. I didn't get to spend nearly as much time at home as I used to. I lost some friends—not because anything bad happened, but because I simply wasn't around. But I knew that I had made the right decision pretty early on in my journey. I'm copy/pasting the following from an essay I published back in 2012 about my experience:

This story takes place in early February of this year. I had woken up at 4:30 a.m. to catch the first flight out of Eastern Iowa so I could be in central Michigan for my talk that evening at a university out there. As far as talks go, it was a good one.

Good energy, great turnout—laughter at my jokes but solemn silence as I explained the struggles of early adolescence—a standing ovation when I finished. After the Q&A and after the mingling, there was only one person left: a young woman my age, who had waited patiently through it all. We’d exceeded our time limit in the auditorium, so we made our way to the lobby, and she explained that she had “recently come to terms with the fact that [she’s] a homosexual.” Now it was my turn to be shocked. I had never heard someone refer to herself like that. But I simply nodded.

She took a deep breath, trembling, trying to maintain her composure. “I always knew I had been different,” she said. “Since I was a little girl, even. And I thought for the longest time I’d never be able to have children—was told I couldn’t. But after listening to you tonight I...” she trailed off, looking away. I realize in retrospect that she simply couldn’t believe she was able to say she was about to tell me. “I can be a mom.” We hugged for a long time—I’ll never forget the look on her face.

What are the most difficult lessons you’ve learned?

One of the most difficult things I had to come to terms with was the "lightning in a bottle" metaphor I mentioned earlier and accepting that many of the factors in my success were utterly beyond my control. Understanding and accepting that helped me ease up on the pressure I was putting on myself to find some new way to top that contribution. Of course, I still want to do more and I'm continuing to fight for the things I believe in. And at the same time, if that speech and my subsequent advocacy work eventually stand as my most valuable professional contributions in life, I am at peace with that.

There are a lot of other things in life I'm excited to do and be a part of that will stand independently from my past work: I'm currently in a graduate program, I'm planning to get married and have children, I hope to write another book and potentially teach, I'm looking forward to continuing to work with my sister on a business venture we started earlier this year, and I plan some day to pursue public service.

I don't feel that any of this in any way fails to live up to the high standards and high expectations set by my past work. A lot of people have projected their hopes or aspirations for my future onto me, but the most important task for me in the here-and-now is to stay focused on being true to myself.

How is dealing with success different than dealing with other experiences?

One unexpected thing I had to deal with was that while my perception of myself didn't really change, other people's perceptions of me changed literally overnight. And bridging that gap hasn't always been easy. I wasn't cruising for fame, and I wasn't looking for a platform. I saw a small opportunity to make a positive contribution, and I took it. But when that video is people's first introduction to me, that isn't necessarily easy for me to deal with.

If you’re having a bad day, how do you keep yourself up?

I often find a cold shower is a good way to hit reset on a bad day. And if that doesn't do the trick, just buck up and do the best you can. We all have bad days from time to time. It's when they start occurring consecutively that you should start to worry a little bit more.

What constitutes a good day?

Generally, a good day is determined by how I feel when I go to bed. If I've made good decisions, made progress on my action lists, had tough and important conversations, I feel really good by the time I go to bed and will usually sleep like a rock. It's the great feeling that's a combination of satisfaction, being tired, and feeling calm about the next day of work.

Who would you like to have coffee with most and why?

Atul Gawande. He's a bestselling author, a surgeon, worked for President Bill Clinton as a healthcare advisor, and a regular correspondent for the New Yorker writing some of the most important work on healthcare today. He's a sharp guy.

On a scale of 1-10, how important is (a) a team (b) a good idea ( c) a strategy (d) organization?

A - 7 B - 5 C - 9 D - 8

If Napoleon Hill was asking you the secret right now, what would you tell him?

"Be prepared." I first learned this lesson in the Boy Scouts and have spent the last two decades learning it over and over again.
