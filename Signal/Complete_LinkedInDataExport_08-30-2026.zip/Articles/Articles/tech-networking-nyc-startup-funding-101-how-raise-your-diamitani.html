In the startup ecosystem, networking events are considered especially important. In New York City, the men and women who attend these events are known as an elite squad called entrepreneurs. These are their recaps.

On Tuesday, October 18, 2016 I had the opportunity to attend an event relating to startup fundraising in New York City. I thought that the key points were useful and I'd love to share them with you.

Great! Now that that's out of the way, let's get down to business. Here are my major takeaways from the panel, hosts and attendees:

Lesson 1: Show Up Looking Good

When you go to raise money, present to partners/potential customers and engage with your community, be prepared and exhibit your best self.

Lesson 2: Get as Much Traction with As Little Money

The guys (and girls) who get the big checks off napkin ideas have usually done it before. They’ve gone through the process of growing and exiting (or seeing tremendous success in) a business and have social capital to put up for collateral.

Lesson 3: Create a Revenue Generating Model

Find out how you’re going to get the first person to start paying you.

Lesson 4: Get Continuous Introductions

Let’s say someone introduces you to an investor, however they don’t respond. Then someone else says they know that same investor. Let them reach out on your behalf. With enough touch points from associates, those intros could turn into dollars.

Lesson 5: Pretend Like You Don’t Need Money

Fundraising is like dating. No one likes the guy or girl who’s too into your love because they don’t have enough of their own. Talk straight, but let it be known that your partnership is a willing one of mutual benefit and not a desperation move.

Lesson 6: Raise Around Milestones

When you build a product or service and you achieve a certain goal, that’s when you’re strong enough to advance towards your next goal – with zero’s in tow.

Lesson 7: Don’t Outsource Fundraising

It can be a mess. It’s your business, do the dirty work.

Lesson 8: Know Your Milestones (Part Two)

Understand how your business operates. Figure out what outcomes would be driven by successful behavior and do your best to perfect your skills and path.

Lesson 9: Don’t Forget Early Stage Investors

The people who helped you out in the beginning might not be covered by legal paperwork or may be shoved aside by the bigger guys when they get in. You have to make sure that they’re taken care of or they run the risk of being mistreated.

Lesson 10: VC’s Are Looking for Reasons to Say No

Make sure you do your best not to give them an easy out. Game theory your life.

Lesson 11: Know What You Don’t Know

Don’t be afraid that you don’t know anything about a term sheet. Just ask someone who does. That knowledge will help you figure out other things you have to learn in order to be successful and building those skills will help you apply them successfully.

Now what? I've gone through my 11 lessons, but I've actually got two more tidbits that could be useful. However, these are not diehard, just some tips from their experiences:

When it comes to the legal work: It's best to pay a lawyer to do it right from the beginning than mess up the process.
When it comes to paying for that legal work: You may be able to get a lawyer to take a fee cap, meaning that they will take less money up front in exchange of the possibility of a payout in later rounds.
When it comes to outsourcing your fundraising: You're the entrepreneur, do it yourself. Sure you can get introductions, but cutting people deals to get you cash can turn shady quickly. And investors get annoyed.
